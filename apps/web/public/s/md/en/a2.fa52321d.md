# Where every number comes from: provenance is a requirement

A figure without an origin can only be accepted or rejected. With its source, its cut-off date and its gaps in plain view, it can be debated.

Most teams know this scene. Someone opens a dashboard, sees a figure they did not expect and asks where it comes from. Nobody knows for sure. The formulas get checked, the author of the report is tracked down, the number is compared against a spreadsheet and, in the end, the meeting is spent finding out whether the figure is true instead of deciding what to do about it.

The idea fits in one sentence: provenance is not a decoration added at the end to make a good impression, but a requirement met before a figure is shown. A number that cannot explain its origin is not data, it is an opinion with formatting.

## A dashboard that demands belief

A traditional dashboard presents clean results: a large figure, a chart, a change against the previous period. That cleanliness is useful for a quick look, but it hides everything needed to doubt with judgment. It does not say whether the number comes from a record, an estimate or a calculation made with assumptions. It does not say which day the data reaches. It does not say which part of the business was left out.

When those answers are missing, the reader has two options, and both are bad. They can accept the figure out of courtesy toward whoever presents it, or reject it out of general distrust. In neither case are they evaluating the number: they are evaluating the person or the tool that shows it.

> A figure that does not explain its origin demands belief. A figure with provenance can be debated.

## Cut-off date, source and gaps

For a figure to be debatable, four things need to travel with it. In Rovaq AI we call them the seal of the figure, and it accompanies the important figures on the screens that show it.

- **The class.** A figure can be observed, meaning a recorded fact; estimated, calculated with a model or with assumptions you define; inferred, derived from other relationships; or it can be No data. Mixing those classes in one column is the most common way to fool yourself without meaning to.
- **The source.** Which file, which system or which load the record comes from, so that anyone in doubt can go and look.
- **The cut-off date.** Which day the data reaches. A figure correct up to the fifteenth and one correct up to the thirtieth are not the same figure, even if they share a name.
- **The age.** How much time has passed since the last update, so nobody mistakes an old snapshot for today.

The rule that holds everything else up is this: if the cut-off date is not known, the seal says so. The same goes for any missing data. What is unknown is shown as No data, never as zero. A zero asserts that something was measured and nothing was found; a missing value admits that it is not known. The difference seems small until someone makes a decision believing a category has no activity when, in fact, nobody loaded its records.

### The ledger that keeps corrections

Provenance also depends on how the data is stored. In Rovaq AI each organization has an insert-only ledger: when something is corrected, a revision is added and the earlier version is not erased. That way, a correction leaves a trail instead of quietly rewriting history, and whoever reviews can see that a change happened and when it was made. The exception is retention: the policy each organization defines can delete data, and that deletion is recorded.

## Data trust versus outside endorsement

Two things that are often confused are worth separating. Data trust answers a practical question: how much can I rely on this figure today. A certification answers another: whether an organization meets a certain standard, verified by a third party. They are different matters and neither replaces the other.

The [data trust](https://rovaq.ai/en/product/data-trust) screen exists for the first. It shows a traffic light per source, the freshness of each figure, its coverage and a list of what is still unknown. It is a tool for everyday work: before moving money or writing to a supplier, whoever decides can see which sources are up to date, which fell behind and which gaps remain open.

The second, certification, is a different conversation: trust in the data is not a certification, and this screen does not try to take that place. What it does is leave the real state of each source in plain view, which is what a team needs to work with figures without going blind.

## A simple test for your own dashboard

You do not need to adopt any tool to start demanding provenance. Take the three figures that weigh most in your decisions and put these questions to them, out loud and in front of whoever prepares them:

1. Which file or system it comes from, and who can open it to check.
2. Which day the data reaches and when it was last updated.
3. Whether it is a recorded fact, an estimate or an inference, and who wrote the assumptions when there are any.
4. Which part of the business was left out and how that absence is being shown.

If the answers arrive quickly and agree with one another, the dashboard earns its place. If they take effort, you already have a list of what is worth fixing before the next meeting. In either case the conversation improves: it stops being about whether the number is true and starts being about what to do with it. A good example is refunds, which we covered in [this article on leaks in returned money](https://rovaq.ai/en/blog/refund-leak-you-can-locate), where every total is worth only as much as its origin.

## What we do not claim

- We do not claim that a figure with provenance is correct. Provenance makes it possible to review it; it does not make it true by itself.
- We do not claim that the data trust screen is a certification or that it replaces an audit.
- We do not claim that the seal accompanies every figure in the product: it accompanies the important figures on the screens that show it.
- We do not promise that data is current at every moment: every figure shows its cut-off date and its age.
