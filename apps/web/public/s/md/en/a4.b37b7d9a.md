# One role, one view: why each role sees only what belongs to it

A tool with everything for everyone looks generous and delivers little. Showing less protects information and helps people work.

When a team adopts a new tool, the first question is usually what it can do. The second, which is almost never asked in time, is who should be able to do what. When that second question is put off, the result is an application where everyone sees everything: the person carrying out a task stumbles over screens that do not concern them, and whoever reviews the money lives alongside controls they do not need.

The thesis runs the other way. Showing each person less is not an uncomfortable restriction but a design decision with two benefits: information is better protected and the work is clearer.

## Fewer screens, more clarity

An unneeded screen has a cost that does not show in demonstrations. Every additional option is something to understand, ignore or avoid touching by mistake. An operator who has to resolve assigned actions gains nothing from seeing the company financial reports; instead, they lose time searching among items that are not theirs. Fewer options also mean less to learn: a new person starts with what they will actually use.

That is why it helps to design views from the work and not from the menu. The question is not which screens exist, but what the person in each role needs to see to do their own job well. Everything else does not appear.

## Four roles with minimal permissions

Rovaq AI works with four roles. Each one receives what its function requires, and nothing more. A well-designed view is one that removes from each person everything they do not need for their work.

- **[Workspace administrator](https://rovaq.ai/en/use-cases/owner).** Configures, decides and approves. Sees the product screens and manages the team, the notices and data retention. This role gives the go-ahead to the actions that are proposed.
- **[Analyst](https://rovaq.ai/en/use-cases/analyst).** Investigates, prepares and proposes. Sees the money, the problems, the pain points and the imports, and imports files. Proposes, but does not approve.
- **[Operator](https://rovaq.ai/en/use-cases/operator).** Carries out the actions assigned to them. Sees the problems, the assigned actions and their evidence, without money figures.
- **[Read-only](https://rovaq.ai/en/use-cases/viewer).** Consults only and cannot edit anything. Sees the screens that apply to it, unable to modify data, connections or decisions.

The separation between whoever proposes and whoever approves deserves its own mention. That the analyst prepares a finding and the workspace administrator approves it is not bureaucracy: it is the way to make sure a different person looks at the evidence before anything is set in motion. And because the operator works without seeing money figures, the person doing the daily work has no access to what they do not use.

This split follows a principle known as least privilege: each person receives only the access their function requires. An account with less reach exposes less if something goes wrong, whether a mistake, an oversight or a credential that falls into the wrong hands.

### A walk-through example

Imagine a shipping problem: several orders on the same route arrive late. The analyst investigates it, gathers the evidence and proposes talking to the carrier. The workspace administrator sees the proposal with its evidence and decides whether to accept it, change it or turn it down. If it is accepted, the operator receives the assigned action and carries it out, with the problem and the evidence in view but without the money figures they do not need. And anyone in the read-only role, if the team uses one, can follow the progress with no risk of changing anything.

Each person sees a different part of the same fact and yet nobody needs a complete view to fulfill their role. That is the simplest proof that the split is well made: nobody misses what they do not see, because they did not need it to work.

## Server-side authorization

There is an important difference between hiding and protecting. Many applications hide from the interface the options a user should not use, and consider the matter settled. But an element that does not appear in the menu still exists if someone knows the address of the screen or the way to ask for the data.

In Rovaq AI authorization is decided on the server. Hiding a menu item is a reading convenience; it is not what protects a screen. The decision of whether a person can see a piece of data or carry out an action is taken on the side the user does not control: always on the server.

### What to ask when evaluating a tool

If you are comparing software for your team, three questions reveal a lot about how it handles permissions:

- **What each role sees, in writing.** A clear table of capabilities is worth more than a general sentence about security.
- **Who approves.** If whoever proposes can also approve, review depends on goodwill and not on design.
- **Where access is decided.** If it is only hidden in the interface, protection is weak; if it is decided on the server, it holds up better.

One question remains that splitting views does not settle on its own: how far to trust what each role reads. That is what our explanation of [the provenance of every number](https://rovaq.ai/en/blog/where-every-number-comes-from) is for: seeing less should not mean knowing less about the origin of what you do see.

## What we do not claim

- We do not claim that minimal permissions eliminate all risk: they reduce what each account can expose, but a person with access can still make a mistake within what their role allows.
- We do not describe here which personal data each role sees: this article covers screens and actions, not the detail of personal data.
